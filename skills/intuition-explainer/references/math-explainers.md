# Mathematical explainers

Prefer CONCRETE CASE → MANIPULATION → PATTERN → CONJECTURE → COUNTEREXAMPLE
OR CONFIRMATION → GENERAL FORM → NOTATION → APPLICATION.

## Construct the claim

Start with objects the learner can build or change. Give variables a visible
meaning before using them symbolically. A theorem can begin with a construction
that maintains its assumptions, followed by an experiment that violates one.

Name the invariant precisely: what is preserved, under which transformation,
with what domain restrictions? A determinant's signed area is different from
its absolute area; a projection formula assumes the specified inner product;
an optimization guarantee may require convexity or a step-size bound.

Finite examples can support a conjecture; they are not a general proof. Separate
visual evidence, numerical approximation, and deductive argument. If the lesson
explains a proof, map each essential step to a construction or invariant and
state the unvisualized assumptions. Do not describe sample agreement as proof.

## Make notation resolve a problem

Introduce one symbolic object at a time after its role is experienced. Show the
equation with live values from the same model and visually link its terms to
the manipulated objects. Explain whether it describes the current state, next
state, an approximation, or a limiting relationship.

For a chain-rule lesson, let a small input displacement pass through two linked
maps. Vary one local gain while fixing the other; only then express the product
of derivatives. Near a nondifferentiable point, show that the assumption fails
rather than printing a smooth tangent anyway.

For probability, distinguish exact distribution values from sampled frequencies.
If sampling is used, provide a reproducible seed and sample size. An expected
value is not a guaranteed outcome. For numerical integration or limiting cases,
show resolution/tolerance and avoid implying floating-point values are exact.

## Extreme cases

Try zero, sign reversal, degeneracy, symmetry, a boundary, and an assumption
violation where meaningful. Test exact formulas with independent hand examples,
finite differences, conservation laws, or alternate derivations. Test the visual
mapping too: coordinate transformations must preserve the claimed geometry.

End with a new construction or reversed question. The learner should predict
what survives and why, not merely substitute numbers into the displayed formula.
